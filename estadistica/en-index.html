Three decisions hold up any research protocol: how far your study can actually go, which claim you are putting to the test, and how much you can trust the number you obtained. This tool walks you through all three, using examples from clinical practice.

Module 1

The scope of the research

Scope is not a box to tick in the protocol: it is the architecture of the study. It defines the depth, the rigor and the final objective, and it follows from two things, what the literature already says and what you actually want to answer. From that single decision come your epidemiological design, your sample size and type, and the measurement and analysis tools you will need.

The resolution spectrum

Think of scope the way you think about the focus of a microscope or the resolution of an imaging study. On the left you see something, but it is blurry. On the right you see exactly what it is and why it is there. Move the dial to travel across it.

Methodological alert: spurious correlations

The risk

Two variables moving together does not mean one affects the other. A spurious correlation is a real mathematical association with no causal link behind it, almost always because a third variable is hiding in the background.

A classic from the wards: patients who receive supplemental oxygen die more often than patients who do not. Oxygen is not killing them; it is prescribed to the sickest patients in the first place. This is called confounding by indication.

How to protect yourself
  • Always ask who else could have caused both things at once (age, severity, comorbidity, socioeconomic status).
  • Check temporal precedence: did the exposure really come before the outcome?
  • Look for biological plausibility, not just a small p value.
  • Remember that adjusting for confounders reduces the problem, it does not eliminate it.
Golden rule: correlation does not imply causation. Only a controlled experimental design lets you speak about causes with any comfort.

A real project travels the whole spectrum

The discovery of Helicobacter pylori as a cause of peptic ulcer disease shows how the same problem changes scope as the evidence matures.

Phase 1 · Exploratory

Something odd on the biopsy

A pathologist notices curved bacteria in gastric biopsies from patients with gastritis, at a time when everyone assumed nothing could survive stomach acid. No literature explains it.

Phase 2 · Descriptive

How common is it?

The organism is cultured and systematically documented: in how many biopsies it appears, in which patients, with which histological findings. Still no claim about causes.

Phase 3 · Correlational

Does it travel with ulcer disease?

Studies of patients with and without ulcers show a strong statistical association between the presence of the bacterium and the disease. Whether it is cause, consequence or coincidence remains open.

Phase 4 · Explanatory

Manipulate in order to prove

Deliberate ingestion of the culture reproduces gastritis, and clinical trials then show that eradicating the bacterium with antibiotics heals the ulcer. Manipulation, control and causation.

Module 2

The hypothesis and how it is tested

A statistical hypothesis is a claim about a characteristic of one or more populations that can be tested with data. In practice you always work with two competing claims, and statistics only helps you decide whether your data are compatible with one of them.

H₀ · null hypothesis
  • States that there is no effect, no difference and no association.
  • It is the hypothesis you try to refute, never the one you confirm.
  • It always includes equality (=, ≤, ≥).
  • Examples: μ treatment = μ placebo, OR = 1, RR = 1, ρ = 0.
H₁ · alternative hypothesis
  • Proposes that an effect, difference or association does exist.
  • It is what the investigator suspects and wants to support.
  • It never includes equality (≠, <, >).
  • Examples: μ treatment ≠ μ placebo, OR > 1, ρ ≠ 0.

Two-tailed or one-tailed: where the rejection region falls

The direction you give H₁ decides where the rejection region sits and how much evidence you will need. Change the controls and watch how α is distributed under the curve.

Careful: switching to a one-tailed test after seeing that the two-tailed one missed significance is p-hacking. Direction is justified before collecting data, with clinical or pathophysiological arguments, and it is written into the protocol.

The five steps of hypothesis testing

Step 1

State H₀ and H₁ in terms of a population parameter, not of your sample.

Step 2

Set α and the test according to the type of variable, the design and the assumptions.

Step 3

Collect the data with the sample size you calculated before starting.

Step 4

Compute the test statistic, its p value and the confidence interval of the effect.

Step 5

Decide: if p < α you reject H₀; if p ≥ α you do not. Then interpret the clinical magnitude.

What never changes

Rejecting H₀ does not prove H₁ is true, and failing to reject it does not prove H₀ is true. You are only saying whether your data are comfortable or uncomfortable under the assumption of no effect.

Module 3

Statistical fundamentals

These are the concepts most often quoted and most often misread in the medical literature. The goal is not to memorize definitions, but to be able to read a result and know what it is telling you and what it is not.

The p value, without the myths

The p value answers exactly one question: if H₀ were true, how likely would it be to observe a result as extreme as mine, or more extreme?

The logic: proof by contradiction
  1. You temporarily assume there is no effect.
  2. You build every possible result under that assumption.
  3. You ask how far from expected your observation landed.

The whole calculation happens inside that hypothetical world. You never abandon the assumption of H₀ in order to compute it.

Example

You treat 10 patients with a new drug and 9 improve within 48 hours, in a condition where spontaneous improvement over that window runs around 50 percent.

p = P(9 or more improvements | 50% spontaneous improvement) ≈ 0.01

By chance alone this would happen once in a hundred times. That is uncomfortable for H₀, not proof that the drug works.

The central confusion, in diagnostic-test language

This is the best available analogy for a medical student, because the mistake is exactly the same one as confusing sensitivity with positive predictive value.

What the p value does answer

P(extreme data | H₀ true)

This is the equivalent of sensitivity: the probability of seeing a certain result given a state of the world we have already assumed.

Correct reading of p = 0.03: if the treatment had no effect at all, results like mine would show up in 3 out of every 100 studies.

What the p value does not answer

P(H₀ true | data)

That would be the positive predictive value, and computing it requires the prior prevalence, that is, how plausible the hypothesis was before the study.

Incorrect reading of p = 0.03: there is a 3 percent chance that I am wrong. The p value does not say that.

Why this matters clinically: just as a highly sensitive test produces many false positives when the disease is rare, a p < 0.05 applied to an implausible hypothesis produces findings that later fail to replicate. The prior plausibility of your hypothesis counts too.

Three steps to read any result

1 · Is there a signal?

Compare p with α. If p < α there is statistical evidence. If p ≥ α there is not enough evidence, which is not the same as evidence that no effect exists.

2 · How large is it?

Compute the effect size (d, r, η², OR, mean difference). A tiny p with a huge n can correspond to a trivial effect. The p value depends on n; the effect size does not.

3 · How precise is it?

Look at the confidence interval. Wide means a lot of uncertainty; narrow means a precise estimate. This is the information most often lost when you read only the p value.

An example worth discussing in class: an antihypertensive lowers systolic pressure with p = 0.001, but the effect is 0.3 mmHg with a 95% CI of 0.1 to 0.5. The p value says there is a signal. The effect size and the interval say it changes nothing clinically.

Confidence level and confidence interval

Confidence level (1 − α)

The probability that the procedure makes the correct decision of not rejecting H₀ when H₀ is true. It is set before the study begins.

ConfidenceαUsual context
90%0.10Pilot and exploratory studies
95%0.05Convention in most clinical literature
99%0.01Trials with hard endpoints, multiple analyses
99.9%0.001Genomic and large-scale association studies

Key relationship: lowering α reduces the risk of a type I error, but you lose power unless you adjust the sample size.

Confidence interval

The range of values compatible with your data, within which the true population parameter is estimated to lie.

CI = x̄ ± z(σ/√n)  or  x̄ ± t(s/√n)

  • A 95% CI means that if you repeated the study many times, about 95 out of every 100 intervals would contain the true parameter.
  • Wider interval, more uncertainty.
  • Larger n, narrower interval and a more precise estimate.
  • If the CI of a difference includes 0, or the CI of an OR includes 1, the result is compatible with no effect.

Type I error in the clinic

Equivalent to a screening test flagging a healthy patient as diseased.

Consequences: unnecessary confirmatory workups, treatments without indication, patient anxiety, unjustified spending and, at the level of the literature, recommendations that are later reversed.

Type II error in the clinic

Equivalent to a test that misses the disease in someone who truly has it.

Consequences: an intervention that did work gets discarded, treatment is delayed and the problem is underestimated. This is the classic error of underpowered studies.

A permanent tension: reducing α increases β if everything else stays the same. The only way to improve both at once is to increase n or reduce the variability of your measurements.

Power and sample size

Power (1 − β) is the probability of detecting a real effect when it exists. The minimum accepted convention is 0.80, and it is computed before the first data point is collected, not afterwards.

Sample size

Larger n, greater power. This is the lever you control most.

Effect size

Large effects are detected with fewer patients.

Level of α

A more permissive α raises power, at the cost of more false positives.

Variability

Lower variance, greater power. Measuring well is also design.

Variables and measurement scales

Quantitative

Discrete: countable whole numbers. Number of hospital admissions, number of seizures per month.

Continuous: any value within a range. Weight, height, serum creatinine, prothrombin time.

Qualitative

Nominal: categories with no order. Blood type, hospital service, causative organism.

Ordinal: categories with order but unequal distances. Tumor stage, NYHA functional class, pain scale.

ScaleWhat it allowsValid statisticsClinical example

Why it matters: the scale determines the test. Comparing medians of an ordinal scale with a Student's t test is one of the most frequent mistakes in undergraduate theses, because it assumes equal distances between categories that do not have them.
